## Summary
An index fund is a mutual fund or ETF that tries to match a market index such as the S&P 500 instead of picking stocks. The SEC explains that passive management can lower costs, and that between two funds with the same holdings, the cheaper one generally returns more, though not every index fund is cheap. S&P's SPIVA scorecard tracks how active managers do against their benchmark: in the first half of 2025, 54% of active large-cap U.S. funds trailed the S&P 500 (65% in full-year 2024), and the institutional scorecard reported at least 80% of equity funds underperformed over 10 years to the end of 2024.

## Practical application
- For most long-term investing, a low-cost broad index fund is a sensible default: check the expense ratio before buying.
- Invest money you won't need for at least 5 years; short-term money belongs in the emergency fund (see [[wealth-emergency-fund]]).
- Automate a monthly contribution and don't try to time the market.
- Prefer tax-advantaged accounts first where they fit (see [[wealth-roth-ira]]).

## Limitations
General education, not personal financial advice. Index funds still lose value when markets fall and can't dodge declines. SPIVA results vary by year and category (mid- and small-cap managers did better in early 2025), and past results do not guarantee future returns.
