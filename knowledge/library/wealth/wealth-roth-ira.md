## Summary
A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax money: contributions are not deductible, but qualified withdrawals are tax-free, and the owner can leave money in it for life (IRS). Roth and traditional IRAs share one yearly limit. For 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67): the IRA limit is $7,500 ($1,100 extra catch-up at 50+); the 401(k) employee limit is $24,500; Roth contributions phase out at modified AGI of $153,000-$168,000 single and $242,000-$252,000 married filing jointly.

## Practical application
- At 20 with income well under the phase-out, a Roth IRA is a strong first retirement account: decades of tax-free growth.
- You need earned income (videography income counts) to contribute; you cannot put in more than you earned.
- Inside the Roth, buy low-cost index funds (see [[wealth-index-funds]]); an IRA is just the account, not the investment.
- Build the emergency fund first (see [[wealth-emergency-fund]]) and set money aside for self-employment tax (see [[wealth-self-employment-tax]]).
- As the business grows, self-employed plans (SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k)) allow much higher contributions; ask a CPA which fits.

## Limitations
General education, not personal tax or financial advice. Limits change every year; this doc must be re-checked when the IRS publishes 2027 limits (late 2026). Withdrawal rules (five-year rule, age 59½, exceptions) are in IRS Publication 590-B and are not covered here.
